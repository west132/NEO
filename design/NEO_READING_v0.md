# Neo — Reading note: character-driven story and scene craft (v0)

Design reading for Neo. Not rules. Each idea is said in plain words, then tagged with the Neo layer it feeds:

- **WORLD** — what happens and why (fixed core + development from the PC's moves and triggers)
- **GM** — what gets shown, when, and where the player gets control back

Ideas are paraphrased from well-known writing and tabletop GM craft. Where an idea **does not fit** Neo, it is marked ✗ with the reason.

---

## 1. Two kinds of story

**Plot-driven:** the author plans events first (beats, twists, a climax at a set point), then moves characters through them.
✗ Doesn't fit. It decides what happens before anyone chooses.

**Character-driven** (also "organic" or "discovery" writing): the author sets up people with wants, puts them in a situation, and asks "what does each one do now?" The plot is what comes out.
✓ This is Neo. The background is the situation; play is finding out what happens.

Stephen King describes his own method this way: put characters in a predicament and watch how they get out, rather than plot first. That is the same move as Neo's world-first idea.

→ **WORLD:** develop the unwritten only by asking what a character would do next, given what they want and what just happened. Never by asking what the story needs.

---

## 2. Want, obstacle, action

Every character-driven story runs on three things per person: **what they want**, **what stands in the way**, **what they do about it**. Conflict comes from wants colliding, not from the author adding trouble.

→ **WORLD:** every actor who matters has a want and a next move (v6.3 already has this as drives + plan + due). The engine's job is to keep those moves running even when the PC isn't watching.
→ **WORLD:** trouble that reaches the PC must come from someone's want or a real cause. Never added because things felt slow.

---

## 3. A scene exists only if something can change

A scene is worth showing when something in it can change: a relationship, a danger, what someone knows, who holds what. Robert McKee frames it as each scene turning some value from one state to another (safe → in danger, trust → doubt). If nothing turns, it isn't a scene. It's a summary line, or nothing.

→ **GM:** before showing a moment as a scene, check: can something change here? No → one line, or skip.
→ This is your "skip scenes where nothing happens" and "routine stays off-screen," given a reason.

---

## 4. Enter late, leave early

A screenwriting habit: start a scene just before the interesting part, end it once its question is answered. Don't show the walk to the door. Don't linger after the deal is struck.

→ **GM:** open a scene at the point where the world meets the PC (or the PC arrives to act). Close it when what could change has changed, or the player faces a choice.
→ Matches v6.2's opening rule (skip to where the world reaches the PC), applied to every scene, not only the first.

---

## 5. Scene and sequel (action, then reaction)

Dwight Swain split stories into two alternating units:

- **Scene:** goal → conflict → outcome (often a setback).
- **Sequel:** reaction → thinking it over → decision, which sets the next goal.

The rhythm of a story comes from swinging between doing and deciding.

→ **GM:** the "sequel" moment is where the player gets control back. A real decision stop is the sequel's decision point.
→ **GM:** the player does the reacting and deciding. The GM never writes the PC's feelings or conclusions.
→ This gives one clear stop rule: stop when a scene's outcome lands and the PC has a real choice; don't stop inside a scene the player's orders already cover.

---

## 6. Show, don't tell

Show what can be seen and heard; let the reader draw the conclusion. Don't explain motives or hidden meaning.

→ **GM:** narrate only what the PC can perceive. Hidden causes appear as signs, never as explanation. (v6.3 already says this in narration craft; story writing gives the reason: the reader's own inference is what makes it feel real.)

---

## 7. Chekhov's gun, and why Neo breaks it

The rule: if a gun is on the wall in act one, it must fire by act three. Everything placed must pay off.

✗ Doesn't fit as a rule. It's plot-driven: it turns every detail into a promise.
→ **WORLD:** in Neo, things exist because the world has them. A gun on the wall may never fire. A clue may never be found. That's what makes the world real instead of staged (your M7: unresolved is valid).
→ The useful part to keep: **don't place a detail with fake weight.** Emphasis follows what's actually noticeable, never what's secretly important.

---

## 8. Ideas from tabletop GM craft

Tabletop designers face the same problem as Neo: a story with a player who can do anything.

**Play to find out what happens** (Apocalypse World's principle). The GM doesn't know the ending and doesn't steer toward one.
→ **WORLD:** confirms Neo's stance. The background fixes truths, never outcomes.

**Say yes, or roll the dice** (Dogs in the Vineyard). If nothing interesting rides on an action, just let it happen. If something does, roll and live with the result.
→ **RULES / GM:** this is your FAST vs roll split in one line: obvious → yes, uncertain → roll, impossible → no.

**Threats with warning signs** (Apocalypse World "fronts"; Blades in the Dark "clocks"). Dangers have a plan and visible stages, so the world moves forward on its own and players can see trouble coming.
→ **WORLD:** this is your pressures, tracks and clocks. Story craft adds one point: each stage must leave a sign someone could notice, or the player never feels the world move.

**Cut to the action, then downtime** (Blades in the Dark). Play jumps straight into the job; recovery, shopping and planning happen in a separate between-jobs phase.
→ **GM:** a model for the between-scenes step: time runs, the world acts, the PC rests or prepares, and only what matters gets a line.

**"Fail forward"** (common GM advice: a failed roll still moves the story on).
✗ Partly doesn't fit. Neo's failures are real and persist (I11). Keep only the true part: a failure changes the situation (worse position, cost, lost chance). It never means "nothing happens, roll again."

---

## 9. The no-help rule, stated in story terms

Writers call it **deus ex machina** ("god from the machine"): a problem solved by something that appears from nowhere. Readers feel cheated because nothing in the world caused it.

→ **WORLD:** Neo forbids it outright. A person, thing or event that exists only to help the PC is never developed. Help exists only if:
1. the background designed it, or
2. the player asks and the world answers with a roll at its real odds (rare here = hard), or
3. it would exist anyway, whether or not the PC needed it.

Otherwise, no.

Test: **would this exist if the PC didn't need it?**

Edge, to confirm: item points remain allowed. They're the PC's own resource spent on their own preparation, not help from the GM.

---

## 10. What this means for the Neo flow

Putting the pieces together, a story-shaped, world-first flow:

1. **Between scenes:** time runs; every actor's move comes due and resolves wherever they are (WORLD).
2. **A scene opens** where the world reaches the PC, or where the PC goes to act. Enter late (GM).
3. **The scene plays:** the PC acts, others act from their own wants, uncertain things are rolled (RULES + WORLD).
4. **The scene closes** when something has changed or a real choice lands. Leave early (GM).
5. **Sequel:** the player reacts and decides. Control is theirs (GM).
6. Back to 1.

The unit of play becomes the **scene**, not the round.

Open question for the design record: where saves and the round count fit once the scene is the unit.
